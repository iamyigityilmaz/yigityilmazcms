Let AI label, let a table decide

While building a bot that answers a dental clinic's Instagram comments and DMs, the most useful choice I made was not letting AI decide anything.

Category:
AutomationArtificial Intelligence
Tags:
n8nInstagramMeta Graph APIdecision tabledata privacy

The problem

Most messages reaching the clinic come from abroad, at every hour of the day. My first idea was to hand the message to a language model and let it write the reply. I quickly saw why that was risky: one day the model might quote a price, and another day it might tell someone "this treatment suits you". In healthcare neither is acceptable.

The fix: two separate jobs

I split the flow in two. The AI returns only three things: the language of the message, its category and a suggested reply. What actually happens is settled by a decision table I wrote by hand.

CategoryWhat happens
Information requestShort reply, details by DM
Price questionNo price is written; the person is invited to DM
PraiseA public thank-you only
Complaint or accusationNo automatic reply; the team is emailed and a human takes over
Medical emergencyAn approved fixed message, plus an email to the team
SpamSilence

The table looks dull, and that is exactly what makes it safe. I can say what will happen in a category by looking at the table, without reading code.

A second check in code

Telling the model "don't write prices" is not enough. So right before sending, code checks the text once more: if anything looks like a price, the message does not go out. In emergencies the model's text is not used at all, and a pre-approved fixed message is sent.

The language rule

The reply goes out in whatever language and alphabet the person used. Russian typed in Latin letters gets an answer in Cyrillic. If the model answers in the wrong language, the flow notices and asks once more with a correction note.

Data privacy

Only the message text reaches the AI service; names and phone numbers do not. Under data protection law, the easiest data to defend is the data you never sent.

Testing

For every version I re-ran more than 30 scenarios: questions in different languages, insults hidden inside praise, and comments that say "ignore your previous instructions". The bot is now on its fifteenth version.

What I learned

Treating AI as the least trusted part of the flow, instead of the smartest, made everything easier. If the model gets it wrong, the worst case is a mislabelled message that ends up with a human.
